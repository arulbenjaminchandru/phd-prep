Research and its types

Research is a systematic, objective search for new knowledge or for answers to a defined question. Good research is systematic, logical, empirical, replicable and ethical.

  • Basic (fundamental, new theory) vs applied (solves a practical problem)
  • Descriptive (describes what is) vs analytical (evaluates using existing facts)
  • Quantitative (numbers, statistics) vs qualitative (meanings, themes); both together = mixed methods
  • Conceptual (ideas, theory) vs empirical (observation, experiment)
  • Exploratory (finds the questions) vs explanatory/causal (tests cause and effect)
  • Cross-sectional (one point in time) vs longitudinal (same subjects over time)

The research process (Kothari)

  1. Formulate the research problem
  2. Extensive literature survey
  3. Develop the hypothesis
  4. Prepare the research design
  5. Determine the sample design
  6. Collect the data
  7. Execute the project
  8. Analyse the data
  9. Test the hypothesis
  10. Generalise and interpret
  11. Prepare the report

Research methods are the techniques you use; research methodology is the reasoning behind choosing them.

Problem, questions and hypotheses

  • Research problem: a gap or difficulty stated precisely. Check it is researchable, significant, feasible (time, data, skills) and ethical.
  • Research question: what you will answer, e.g. “Does mutation feedback raise the fault-detection rate of LLM-written tests?”
  • Hypothesis: a testable prediction. H0 (null): no effect or difference. H1 (alternative): an effect exists.
  • A good hypothesis is clear, specific, testable, stated before data collection and consistent with known facts.
  • Directional hypothesis → one-tailed test; non-directional → two-tailed.

Variables

  • Independent: what you change (e.g. prompting strategy)
  • Dependent: what you measure (e.g. mutation score)
  • Control: kept constant (same projects, same time budget)
  • Extraneous / confounding: uncontrolled factors that distort results (e.g. a model has already seen the benchmark)
  • Moderating: changes the strength of a relationship. Mediating: explains how the effect happens.

Research design

  • Exploratory (formulative): flexible; literature, expert surveys, case studies.
  • Descriptive / diagnostic: accurate description; surveys, observation.
  • Experimental (hypothesis-testing): manipulate the independent variable. A true experiment needs random assignment and a control group; a quasi-experiment lacks random assignment.
  • Fisher's principles of experimental design: replication, randomisation, local control.
  • Others: case study, ex post facto (after the event), action research, survey research.

Sampling error comes from studying a sample and shrinks as n grows. Non-sampling error (measurement, response, processing mistakes) can happen even in a census.

Collecting data

  • Primary (first-hand): questionnaire (filled by respondents), schedule (filled by an enumerator), interview (structured, semi-structured, unstructured), observation (participant or non-participant), experiment, focus group.
  • Secondary (collected by others): reports, published datasets, logs, repositories such as GitHub or Defects4J.
  • Pilot study: a small trial run to test the instrument.
  • Avoid leading and double-barrelled questions; mix open and closed questions on purpose.

Reliability and validity

  • Reliability = consistency: test–retest, parallel forms, split-half, internal consistency (Cronbach's α; 0.7 or more is usually acceptable), inter-rater.
  • Validity = measures what it claims: content, construct (convergent, discriminant), criterion (concurrent, predictive), face.
  • A measure can be reliable without being valid; a valid measure must be reliable.
  • Internal validity: is the effect really caused by the treatment? External validity: does it generalise?
  • Software engineering papers discuss threats to internal, external, construct and conclusion validity (Wohlin et al.).

Descriptive statistics

mean x̄ = Σx ÷ n · median = middle value · mode = most frequent
s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) · SD = √s² · CV = SD ÷ mean × 100
  • The mean is pulled by outliers; the median is robust.
  • Right (positive) skew: mean > median. Left skew: mean < median.
  • Normal curve: 68% within ±1 SD, 95% within ±1.96 SD, 99.7% within ±3 SD.
  • Standard error = SD ÷ √n; 95% confidence interval ≈ x̄ ± 1.96 × SE.
  • Pearson r (linear, interval/ratio data) and Spearman ρ (ranks) both run from −1 to +1. Correlation is not causation.
  • Regression y = a + bx; R² is the share of variance in y explained by x.

Hypothesis testing

Steps: state H0 and H1 → choose α (usually 0.05) → choose the test → compute the statistic and p-value → reject H0 if p < α → interpret.

H0 trueH0 false
Reject H0Type I error (α), false positiveCorrect (power = 1 − β)
Keep H0CorrectType II error (β), false negative
  • The p-value is the chance of a result at least this extreme if H0 were true. It is not the chance that H0 is true.
  • Bigger samples give more power. Report effect sizes too: Cohen's d, or Vargha–Delaney Â12 in testing research.
  • Degrees of freedom: one-sample t → n − 1; chi-square independence → (r − 1)(c − 1).

Literature review

  • Purpose: show what's known, find the gap, justify your question and method.
  • Sources: journals, conference proceedings, theses, books, preprints (arXiv), standards. Primary (original study) vs secondary (review) vs tertiary (index, encyclopedia).
  • Databases: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, DBLP.
  • Systematic literature review: protocol → search strings → inclusion/exclusion → quality assessment → data extraction → synthesis (Kitchenham's guidelines in software engineering; PRISMA flow diagram).
  • Snowballing: backward (a paper's references) and forward (papers citing it).
  • Reference managers: Zotero, Mendeley, JabRef (BibTeX).

Writing and citing

  • Paper structure IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, plus abstract, conclusion and references.
  • Thesis: introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion, conclusion and future work, references, appendices.
  • Abstract (about 150–250 words): problem, method, results, conclusion.
  • Citation styles: IEEE (numbered [1], common in computing), APA (author–date), Harvard, MLA, Chicago.
  • References = works cited; bibliography = works consulted.
  • Ibid. = same source as the previous note; op. cit. = the work already cited; et al. = and others.

Research and publication ethics

  • Misconduct = FFP: fabrication (inventing data), falsification (manipulating data or methods), plagiarism (using others' work without credit).
  • Also misconduct: duplicate publication, salami slicing, gift or ghost authorship, undisclosed conflicts of interest, citation manipulation.
  • Plagiarism forms: verbatim, paraphrase without credit, mosaic (patchwriting), idea plagiarism, self-plagiarism.

Journals, indexing and metrics

  • Peer review: single-blind (reviewers anonymous), double-blind (both anonymous), open.
  • Indexing: Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate: SCIE, SSCI, ESCI), UGC-CARE list (India), DBLP (computing).
  • Impact factor (Clarivate): citations this year to items from the previous two years ÷ citable items published in those two years. CiteScore (Scopus) uses a four-year window.
  • h-index: h papers with at least h citations each. i10-index (Google Scholar): papers with 10 or more citations.
  • Open access: gold (publisher, often with a fee), green (self-archived copy or preprint), diamond (free for authors and readers).
  • Predatory signs: guaranteed fast acceptance, fees before review, fake impact factors, spam invitations. Check Scopus, Web of Science or UGC-CARE first.
  • ISSN = journals, ISBN = books, DOI = permanent link to an article.

Intellectual property in India

  • Patent: new, inventive and industrially applicable; 20 years from filing (Patents Act, 1970). A computer programme “per se” is not patentable (Section 3(k)).
  • Copyright: protects expression, including software code; for literary works, the author's life plus 60 years.
  • Also: trademarks, industrial designs, geographical indications (e.g. Kanchipuram silk).
  • Open-source licences: MIT and Apache 2.0 are permissive; GPL is copyleft (derived work must stay GPL).

Process models

  • Waterfall: sequential phases; suits stable requirements; testing comes late.
  • V-model: each development phase paired with a test level (requirements ↔ acceptance, design ↔ system/integration, code ↔ unit).
  • Incremental / iterative: deliver in pieces.
  • Prototyping: a quick model to clarify requirements (throwaway or evolutionary).
  • Spiral (Boehm): risk-driven loops of planning, risk analysis, engineering, evaluation.
  • Agile Manifesto (2001): 4 values, 12 principles. DevOps: CI/CD pipelines automate build, test and deploy.

Agile and Scrum

  • Roles: Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers.
  • Events: Sprint (one month or less), Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum (15 min), Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective.
  • Artifacts: Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment.
  • XP practices: pair programming, test-driven development (red → green → refactor), continuous integration, small releases.
  • Kanban: visualise work, limit work in progress.
  • User story: “As a <role>, I want <goal> so that <benefit>.” Velocity = story points per sprint.

Requirements and design

  • Functional (what the system does) vs non-functional (how well: performance, security, usability).
  • SRS standard: IEEE 830, now ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148. A traceability matrix links requirements to tests.
  • Cohesion (high is good), best → worst: functional, sequential, communicational, procedural, temporal, logical, coincidental.
  • Coupling (low is good), best → worst: data, stamp, control, external, common, content.
  • SOLID: single responsibility, open/closed, Liskov substitution, interface segregation, dependency inversion.
  • UML: use case, class, sequence, activity, state machine, component, deployment diagrams.
  • Design patterns (Gang of Four, 23): creational (Singleton, Factory), structural (Adapter, Decorator), behavioural (Observer, Strategy).

  • MTBF = MTTF + MTTR; availability = MTTF ÷ (MTTF + MTTR).

Testing levels and types

  • Verification: are we building the product right? Validation: are we building the right product?
  • Error (human mistake) → fault, defect or bug (in code) → failure (wrong behaviour at run time).
  • Levels: unit → integration → system → acceptance.
  • Integration: big-bang, top-down (uses stubs), bottom-up (uses drivers), sandwich.
  • Acceptance: alpha (at the developer's site), beta (real users at their site), UAT.
  • Smoke (does the build basically work?), sanity (narrow check after a fix), regression (did changes break old features?), exploratory.
  • Static testing (reviews, inspections, walkthroughs, static analysis) vs dynamic testing (running code).
  • Seven principles (ISTQB): testing shows presence of defects, exhaustive testing is impossible, test early, defects cluster, pesticide paradox, context dependent, absence-of-errors fallacy.

Black-box techniques

  • Equivalence partitioning: one test per class of inputs that should behave alike (valid and invalid classes).
  • Boundary value analysis: test at and around the edges. For n inputs: basic 4n + 1 tests, robust 6n + 1, worst-case 5n.
  • Example: valid age 18–60 → test 17, 18, 19, 59, 60, 61.
  • Decision tables: combinations of conditions → actions. Also cause–effect graphs, state transitions, use cases.
  • Pairwise (combinatorial): cover every pair of parameter values with few tests.
  • Error guessing and exploratory testing rely on experience.

White-box testing and coverage

  • 100% path coverage ⇒ 100% branch (decision) coverage ⇒ 100% statement coverage. Each stronger criterion subsumes the weaker.
  • Condition coverage: each Boolean sub-condition is true and false. MC/DC: each condition shown to change the decision on its own (required for the most critical avionics software, DO-178C Level A).
Cyclomatic complexity V(G) = E − N + 2P = decision points + 1 = regions
  • V(G) is the number of independent paths, the minimum tests for basis-path coverage. One if-else plus one while loop → V(G) = 3.
  • Data-flow testing covers def–use pairs.
  • Coverage tools: JaCoCo (Java), coverage.py (Python), Istanbul (JavaScript).

Mutation, oracles, flakiness, prioritisation

  • Mutation testing: inject small faults (mutants), e.g. change > to >=, + to −, delete a statement. A test that fails on a mutant kills it.
Mutation score = killed ÷ (total − equivalent) × 100
  • Equivalent mutant: behaves exactly like the original, so no test can kill it. Detecting them is undecidable in general.
  • Justified by the competent-programmer hypothesis and the coupling effect. Tools: PIT (Java), mutmut (Python), Stryker (JavaScript).
  • Test oracle: decides whether an output is correct. Kinds: specified (from specs), derived (earlier versions, metamorphic relations), implicit (crashes, exceptions), human.
  • Metamorphic testing: check relations between outputs, e.g. sin(x) = sin(π − x).
  • Flaky test: passes and fails on the same code. Causes: async waits, concurrency, test order, network, time, randomness.
APFD = 1 − (TF₁ + … + TFₘ) ÷ (n × m) + 1 ÷ (2n)

n = tests, m = faults, TFᵢ = position of the first test that reveals fault i. Higher is better.

Automated test generation (her field)

  • Random / feedback-directed: Randoop (Java).
  • Search-based: EvoSuite (Java; a genetic algorithm evolves whole test suites), Pynguin (Python).
  • Symbolic / concolic execution: KLEE; solve path constraints to reach branches.
  • Fuzzing: AFL, libFuzzer; mutate inputs to find crashes.
  • Model-based testing: tests from state machines or specifications.
  • LLM-based: prompt a code model with the class under test. Meta's TestGen-LLM keeps only tests that build, pass reliably and add coverage.
  • Benchmarks: Defects4J (real Java bugs), BugsInPy (real Python bugs), SWE-bench (real GitHub issues for coding agents).
  • Frameworks: JUnit, pytest; mocking: Mockito, unittest.mock.

Graphs, paradigms and NP

  • BFS / DFS: O(V + E). BFS gives shortest paths in unweighted graphs; DFS finds cycles and topological order.
  • Dijkstra: O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap; no negative edges. Bellman–Ford: O(VE); handles negative edges and detects negative cycles. Floyd–Warshall: all pairs, O(V³).
  • Minimum spanning tree: Kruskal O(E log E) with union–find; Prim O(E log V).
  • Greedy: Huffman coding, fractional knapsack, activity selection. Dynamic programming: 0/1 knapsack, LCS, matrix-chain multiplication. Divide and conquer: merge sort, quicksort, binary search.
  • Master theorem, T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n): merge sort 2T(n/2) + n → Θ(n log n); binary search T(n/2) + 1 → Θ(log n).
  • P ⊆ NP. NP-complete: SAT, 3-SAT, vertex cover, clique, Hamiltonian cycle, subset sum. TSP optimisation is NP-hard.

Databases: keys and normal forms

  • Super key ⊇ candidate key (minimal) → primary key (the one chosen); alternate keys; a foreign key references another table's key.
  • 1NF atomic values. 2NF no partial dependency on part of a composite key. 3NF no transitive dependency (for X → A, X is a superkey or A is a key attribute). BCNF every determinant is a superkey. 4NF no multi-valued dependency. 5NF no join dependency.
  • BCNF is stricter than 3NF; a 3NF decomposition can always preserve dependencies, BCNF may not.
  • ER model: entities, attributes (simple, composite, multivalued, derived), relationships (1:1, 1:N, M:N), weak entities.
  • Armstrong's axioms: reflexivity, augmentation, transitivity.

SQL and relational algebra

  • DDL (CREATE, ALTER, DROP), DML (SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE), DCL (GRANT, REVOKE), TCL (COMMIT, ROLLBACK, SAVEPOINT).
  • WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after GROUP BY.
  • Logical order: FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY.
  • Joins: inner, left/right/full outer, self, cross (Cartesian).
  • DELETE removes chosen rows (WHERE allowed); TRUNCATE removes all rows quickly; DROP removes the table itself.
  • Relational algebra: σ select (rows), π project (columns), ⋈ join, ∪ ∩ − set operations, × Cartesian product, ρ rename, ÷ division (“for all”).

Operating systems: scheduling

  • States: new → ready → running → waiting → terminated. A context switch saves and restores the PCB. Threads share their process's memory.
turnaround = completion − arrival · waiting = turnaround − burst · response = first run − arrival
  • FCFS (convoy effect); SJF / SRTF (lowest average waiting time, needs burst estimates, can starve); priority (starvation, fixed by aging); round robin (time quantum, good response); multilevel feedback queue.
  • Worked example, bursts 5, 3, 1 all arriving at 0. FCFS waits 0, 5, 8 → average 4.33. SJF runs 1, 3, 5 → waits 0, 1, 4 → average 1.67.

Operating systems: synchronisation and deadlock

  • A critical-section solution needs mutual exclusion, progress and bounded waiting (Peterson's solution for two processes).
  • Semaphore: wait (P) decrements, signal (V) increments. A binary semaphore works like a mutex.
  • Classic problems: producer–consumer, readers–writers, dining philosophers.
  • Deadlock needs all four Coffman conditions: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait.
  • Handling: prevention (break a condition), avoidance (Banker's algorithm keeps a safe state), detection and recovery, or ignore it (ostrich).
  • Starvation is not deadlock. Livelock: processes keep changing state without progress.

Operating systems: memory and disks

  • Paging: fixed-size pages, no external fragmentation, some internal. Segmentation: variable-size, external fragmentation.
  • The TLB caches page-table entries.
EAT = h × (t + m) + (1 − h) × (t + 2m)

h = TLB hit ratio, t = TLB time, m = memory access time.

  • Page replacement: FIFO (suffers Belady's anomaly), LRU, Optimal (replace the page used farthest in future; fewest faults but not implementable), LFU.
  • Thrashing: too little memory per process → constant page faults. Fix with the working-set model or fewer processes.
  • Disk scheduling: FCFS, SSTF, SCAN (elevator), C-SCAN, LOOK.

Networks: layers and protocols

  • OSI (7): physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application. TCP/IP (4): link, internet, transport, application.
  • Hub (layer 1), switch (layer 2, MAC addresses), router (layer 3, IP addresses).
  • TCP: connection-oriented and reliable; 3-way handshake SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK; flow control with a sliding window; congestion control (slow start, congestion avoidance, fast retransmit). UDP: connectionless, no guarantees, low overhead (DNS, streaming, VoIP).
  • ARP maps IP → MAC; DNS maps names → IP; DHCP assigns addresses; NAT shares one public IP.
  • Error detection: parity, checksum, CRC. Hamming code corrects single-bit errors.

Compiler design

  • Phases: lexical analysis (tokens; regular expressions and finite automata) → syntax analysis (parse tree; context-free grammar) → semantic analysis (types, scopes) → intermediate code (e.g. three-address code) → optimisation → code generation. The symbol table and error handler serve every phase.
  • Top-down parsers: recursive descent, LL(1) (no left recursion, left-factored; built from FIRST and FOLLOW sets).
  • Bottom-up (shift–reduce): LR(0) < SLR(1) < LALR(1) < CLR(1) in power. YACC builds LALR(1) parsers.
  • Optimisations: constant folding, dead-code elimination, common sub-expression elimination, loop-invariant code motion, strength reduction.

Machine learning essentials

  • Supervised (labelled: classification, regression), unsupervised (clustering, PCA), semi-supervised, reinforcement (agent, environment, reward, policy).
  • Split into train, validation and test sets; k-fold cross-validation.
  • Overfitting: great on training data, poor on new data (high variance). Underfitting: poor on both (high bias). Fixes: more data, L1/L2 regularisation, dropout, early stopping, simpler models.
precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP) · recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN) · F1 = 2PR ÷ (P + R)
  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) ÷ all. Prefer precision and recall when classes are imbalanced, e.g. few buggy files.
  • Regression metrics: MAE, MSE, RMSE, R².
  • Algorithms: linear and logistic regression, decision trees (information gain, Gini), random forest (bagging), gradient boosting, SVM (maximum margin, kernels), k-NN, naive Bayes, k-means.
  • Uses in quality assurance: defect prediction, test prioritisation, flaky-test detection.

Deep learning and LLMs

  • Neuron: weighted sum + activation (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, softmax). Training: forward pass, loss, backpropagation, gradient descent.
  • CNNs for images; RNN/LSTM for sequences; Transformers use self-attention (“Attention Is All You Need”, 2017).
  • LLMs are transformers pre-trained to predict the next token on large text and code corpora, then instruction-tuned.
  • Token, context window, temperature (higher = more random), top-p.
  • Prompting: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought. Adapting: fine-tuning, LoRA.
  • Embeddings: vectors that put similar meanings close together (cosine similarity). RAG: retrieve relevant documents or code and add them to the prompt.
  • Hallucination: fluent but false output, such as calling methods that don't exist. Reduce it with grounding, execution and verification.
  • Open code models: Code Llama, StarCoder, DeepSeek-Coder, Qwen2.5-Coder. Code metric: pass@k. Agents: models that call tools such as a compiler or test runner in a loop.

Slide 7 · about 90 seconds

The approach is a loop around the model. Step one retrieves the right context: the class under test, how it's called, existing tests and documentation, so the model doesn't invent APIs. Step two generates candidate tests with an open code model.

Step three compiles each test and runs it five times; tests that don't build, or that flip between pass and fail, are caught here. Step four runs mutation analysis: small faults are injected, and we see which ones the tests still miss.

Step five sends compiler errors and surviving mutants back into the prompt, so the next round targets what's missing. Finally, step six checks the oracles: when a test fails, it cross-checks the assertion against documentation, earlier versions of the code and other models, to decide whether we've found a real bug or a wrong assertion.

The output is a curated test suite plus a list of suspected bugs for developers.

I want to make AI-written software tests trustworthy. Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can now generate unit tests that read like developer-written code, and industry has begun deploying them. Yet many generated tests fail to compile or run, some pass while checking the wrong behaviour, and industrial pipelines discard failing tests because they cannot tell a real fault from an incorrect assertion. Evaluations are also threatened by benchmark data the models may have seen during training. This research will (1) characterise why LLM-generated tests fail across open models and real projects; (2) design a framework that combines retrieved project context with mutation-analysis feedback to steer generation toward tests that detect faults; (3) add an oracle-validation step that separates genuine fault-revealing failures from wrong assertions; and (4) evaluate the framework on real-fault benchmarks (Defects4J, BugsInPy) and on projects created after the models' training cut-off, against search-based (EvoSuite, Pynguin) and prompting baselines. Expected outcomes are an open-source tool, a leakage-free evaluation dataset and peer-reviewed publications.

  2. Design context retrieval plus mutation feedback that raises fault detection (RQ2).
  3. Automatically separate real-bug failures from wrong assertions (RQ3).
  4. Evaluate on real faults and unseen code against strong baselines, including cost (RQ4).

7What exactly is the research gap?

8What is the test oracle problem?

Deciding whether a program's output for a test input is correct. Oracles can be specified, derived (earlier versions, metamorphic relations), implicit (crashes, exceptions) or human. LLMs can guess oracles, but they may copy the current, possibly buggy, behaviour.

9What is mutation testing, and why not just use coverage?

Inject small deliberate faults such as changing > to >=; good tests fail on them, which kills the mutant. Mutation score = killed ÷ (total − equivalent). Coverage only shows code ran; mutation score shows whether the tests would notice a fault.

10What is an equivalent mutant?

A mutant that behaves exactly like the original, so no test can kill it. Detecting them is undecidable in general (Rice's theorem). Meta's ACH used an LLM agent for it: precision 0.79 and recall 0.47, rising to 0.95 and 0.96 with simple preprocessing.

11How is your work different from Meta's TestGen-LLM and ACH?

Theirs are in-house tools on Meta's Kotlin code with Meta's own models. Yours is open and reproducible, uses open models, targets fault detection and oracle validity, diagnoses failing tests instead of discarding them, and is evaluated on unseen projects.

12Why open models and not ChatGPT?

Reproducibility (fixed model versions), cost, privacy (code stays on the machine) and the option to fine-tune. One commercial model can still be included as a comparison.

13How will you handle hallucination?

Ground the prompt with real project context, execute everything (compile, run repeatedly), feed errors back, and never trust a test that hasn't been run and mutation-checked.

14How will you avoid data leakage?

Report results separately on projects created after each model's training cut-off, collected from repository history with their real fixes.

15What if your approach doesn't beat EvoSuite?

The two are complementary: coverage versus readable, meaningful assertions, so a hybrid is a valid outcome. The failure taxonomy and the leakage-free dataset are contributions on their own, and rigorous negative results are publishable.
